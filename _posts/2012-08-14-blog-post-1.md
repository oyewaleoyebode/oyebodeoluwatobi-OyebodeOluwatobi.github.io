I am pleased to share that our paper, **“Speaker Identification for Low-End Devices: A Secure Voice Biometric Solution for Mobile Banking,”** has been published in the proceedings of the **2025 11th IEEE International Conference on Privacy Computing and Data Security (PCDS)**.

![IEEE PCDS 2025 publication]({{ "/images/blog/pcds-2025-publication.jpg" | relative_url }})

## About the research

Mobile-banking users frequently rely on passwords and personal identification numbers for authentication. However, these credentials can be disclosed, stolen, or shared. The problem is particularly important for users of low-end mobile devices that may not provide dependable fingerprint, facial-recognition, or other dedicated biometric capabilities.

Our research investigates speaker identification as an additional authentication mechanism for such environments. The system combines speech and speaker representations derived from **Whisper** and **ECAPA-TDNN** and includes a liveness-detection component to strengthen protection against non-live voice samples.

The study evaluates whether voice biometrics can support accessible and reliable identity verification across different devices and recording conditions. It also establishes a foundation for our subsequent research on degradation-aware authentication, cancellable speaker credentials, and privacy-preserving encrypted verification.

## Publication information

**Authors:** Oyebode Oluwatobi Oyewale, Yuzo Taenaka, and Youki Kadobayashi

**Conference:** 2025 11th IEEE International Conference on Privacy Computing and Data Security

**Pages:** 80–87

Following the International Symposium on the Origin of Life at Keihanna Plaza, I had the opportunity to visit the **Advanced Telecommunications Research Institute International (ATR)** in Kyoto.

![Visit to ATR Kyoto]({{ "/images/blog/atr-visit-group.jpg" | relative_url }})

ATR conducts research across telecommunications, robotics, artificial intelligence, neuroscience, and human–machine interaction. The visit offered an opportunity to see how interdisciplinary research is translated into experimental systems and specialised research facilities.

One of the most memorable parts of the visit was seeing humanoid receptionist robots designed to communicate and interact with people. These systems illustrated how robotics, language, perception, and behavioural modelling can be combined to create more natural forms of human–machine interaction.

![Humanoid robot at ATR]({{ "/images/blog/atr-humanoid-robot.jpg" | relative_url }})

I also visited ATR’s large anechoic chamber, a specialised environment designed to minimise unwanted acoustic and electromagnetic interference during advanced signal and communication experiments.

![ATR anechoic chamber]({{ "/images/blog/atr-anechoic-chamber.jpg" | relative_url }})

The visit was particularly relevant to my broader interest in secure communication and voice-based technologies. It demonstrated the importance of controlled experimental environments when evaluating speech, signals, communication systems, and interactive technologies.

The experience provided a valuable view of how innovation in robotics and telecommunications is shaping the future of communication between people and intelligent systems.

Across Africa, millions of people rely on mobile phones, USSD services, passwords, and short personal identification numbers to access digital financial services. These technologies have expanded financial inclusion, but they have also created security challenges involving stolen credentials, identity misuse, social engineering, SIM-related fraud, and impersonation.

These risks can be particularly serious in environments where users rely on low-end mobile devices or conduct transactions through agent-assisted and point-of-sale services. Such devices may not provide dependable fingerprint or facial-recognition capabilities, making alternative authentication mechanisms worth investigating.

![Voice biometrics for mobile banking]({{ "/images/blog/voice-mobile-banking.jpg" | relative_url }})

## Why investigate voice authentication?

A person’s voice can provide information that helps distinguish one speaker from another. Voice authentication is also attractive because it can use microphones already available on many mobile and communication devices.

My research at the **Nara Institute of Science and Technology** investigates how voice biometrics could support identity authentication in mobile-banking environments involving different devices, communication channels, and acoustic conditions.

The research examines:

- Speaker identification and verification across different devices
- Voice authentication under noisy and degraded conditions
- Liveness detection for identifying non-live voice samples
- Protection of stored speaker representations
- Cancellable credentials that can be replaced after exposure
- Homomorphic encryption for privacy-preserving verification

## What have we established?

Our experimental speaker-identification system combines representations derived from **Whisper** and **ECAPA-TDNN**. It has been evaluated using speech collected across different devices and indoor and outdoor environments.

The system achieved 99.59% identification accuracy and approximately 99.60% F1-score under the evaluated conditions. We also developed a liveness-detection component for distinguishing live samples from the non-live samples represented in our experiments.

These findings are promising, but they do not yet prove readiness for large-scale banking deployment. Field testing with financial institutions, diverse users, operational threats, communication networks, and regulatory requirements would be necessary before such a system could be adopted.

## Why this research matters

For banks and financial-technology providers, voice authentication could provide an additional layer of identity verification where conventional device-based biometrics are unavailable.

For agent-assisted services, it could help strengthen the connection between a transaction and the identity of the account holder.

For users, it could provide a more accessible authentication option without relying exclusively on knowledge-based credentials such as passwords and PINs.

The objective is not to replace every existing authentication method with voice. A more realistic direction is to determine when voice biometrics can serve as a secure additional factor within a risk-aware authentication framework.

I welcome conversations with researchers, financial institutions, fintech companies, mobile-network operators, and agent-network managers interested in evaluating secure and privacy-preserving voice authentication in realistic African banking environments.
